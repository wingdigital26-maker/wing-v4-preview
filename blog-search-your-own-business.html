Why Am I Not Showing Up When I Search My Own Business?

Searching yourself is the least reliable test in local search. Here is what your phone is really measuring, and how to check properly.

Jack Wing Published August 6, 2026

Two things can be true at once: your phone shows you at the top, and your customers cannot find you at all. Searching your own business is the test almost every owner runs, and it is the one test almost guaranteed to mislead you, in both directions. Before you conclude anything from it, it helps to know what that search is actually measuring.

Why does my own phone show a different result?

Because Google is not answering the question you think you asked. It is answering "what should this specific person, standing in this specific spot, on this specific device, see right now." Google says so plainly in its own explanation of why search results differ: "sometimes, personalization may affect the order that individual results would be ranked on the search results page," and location context shifts results on its own, with or without personalization switched on.

Now consider who is running the search. You have visited your own website more than anyone alive. You are usually standing at your shop or sitting in your own driveway when you check. Your Google account is signed in on the phone in your hand. Every one of those is a thumb on the scale, and they all press in the same direction.

Searching your own business name is like asking your mother whether your haircut looks good. The answer is real. It is just not evidence.

Is incognito enough to get a clean answer?

It helps, and it is not enough. An incognito window drops your account history, which removes one of the three thumbs. It does not move you across town, and location is usually the factor doing the most bending. Google still uses your approximate geographic area, your language, and your device type even with personalization off. So incognito answers "how do I rank from right here," which is a slightly better question than the one you started with, and still not the one that pays.

The three habits worth breaking, since each one flatters you in a different way:

  • Searching from the shop. Distance is a named ranking factor, so standing at your own address is the single most favourable place on earth to test from.
  • Searching your business name. Ranking for your own name only proves Google knows you exist. Nobody who has your name typed into a search bar is a lead you had to earn.
  • Searching while signed in. Google has watched you click your own site for years. It has drawn the obvious conclusion.

How do I check where I actually stand?

Stop testing and start reading the data you already own. The Performance tab inside your Google Business Profile reports the searches real customers used to find you, from wherever they happened to be standing, and it does not care how you feel about it. That is the honest number, and almost nobody looks at it.

It is worth knowing what you are looking for once you get there. Google names exactly three things that decide local ranking in its local ranking guidance: relevance, distance, and prominence. Relevance is how well your profile matches the search. Distance is how far you are from the searcher. Prominence is how well-known you are. If you are missing, you are losing on at least one of those three, and it is usually not the one you assume.

What if I rank at home but not two towns over?

Then nothing is broken, and you have found the ceiling on distance. Ranking falls away as the searcher moves further from your pin, and you cannot pick your business up and move it into Frisco for the afternoon. What you can do is give Google a reason to consider you relevant out there anyway, which is what a real city page is for: a page genuinely about your work in that town, not a copy of your home page with the city name swapped. That is the difference between competing in one suburb and competing across a metro, and it is the slow part of the job.

If you are missing even at your own address, that is a different and more urgent problem, and it is usually one of the five in this post on Maps visibility. Start there.
