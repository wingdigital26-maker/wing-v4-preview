What Should Be on a Roofing Company Website?

A homeowner gives a roofing site about as long as it takes to find the phone number. Make that easy, and make the rest worth reading.

Wing Digital Published October 9, 2026 7 min read

A lot of roofing websites have the same three things: a stock photo of a roof that is not theirs, the word "quality" four times, and a phone number in a font size usually reserved for legal disclaimers.

A roofing company website needs a clear headline saying what you do and where, a tap-to-call phone number on every page, a separate page for each roofing service and each town you work, real photos of your own jobs, visible proof such as reviews and your license or registration number, and pages that load fast on a phone. Everything else is a nice-to-have. Those are what turn a visit into an inspection.

What should a roofing homepage show first?

Before anyone scrolls, the top of the page should answer four questions: what do you do, where, why should I trust you, and how do I reach you right now.

  • A headline that names the job and the area, like "Roof replacement and storm repair in Collin County", not "Excellence in Every Shingle".
  • A phone number you can tap, in the header, on every page, on phones too. Hiding the number to push people to a form costs calls.
  • One button for a free inspection.
  • A real photo of your crew, your truck or a roof you actually did.

Which pages does a roofing website need?

One page per job you want to be found for, and one page per town you want it in. The homepage cannot rank for everything at once.

  • Service pages: roof replacement, roof repair, storm and hail damage inspection, and any material you specialise in.
  • City pages for the towns you really serve, each with something true about that town. Copies with the name swapped help nobody, which we cover in how many city pages a contractor needs.
  • An about page with your license or registration number, insurance, and how long you have been roofing.
  • A contact page with a short form: name, phone, address, what is wrong with the roof.
  • Answers to common questions: cost, how long a replacement takes, what happens after a storm. Two posts a month covering what homeowners ask before they hire is plenty.

What makes homeowners trust a roofer's website?

Proof they can check. Homeowners have heard the storm-chaser stories, so the site's job is to look like the opposite: a local company with a name, a license and a record.

  • Your state roofing license or registration number where your state issues one, in the footer of every page.
  • Reviews with dates, linked to your Google profile. Recent ones matter most: BrightLocal's review survey found most people look for reviews from the last three months.
  • Photos of your own jobs, before and after, with the town named.
  • Plain answers about insurance, without promises. Never offer to cover a deductible.

This matters even if most of your work comes from knocking after a storm. A homeowner who meets you at the door will usually look you up before they call back, and a thin site undoes a good first conversation.

How fast does a roofing website need to load?

Fast enough that someone standing in the yard on a phone does not give up. Google measures this with its Core Web Vitals, which look at how quickly the main content appears, how quickly the page responds to a tap, and whether things jump around while it loads. Big uncompressed photos and stacks of plugins are the usual causes of a slow site.

The phone view is the one that counts. Most site builders show you the desktop version while you edit, so it is easy to end up with a site that looks great on the office monitor and makes a homeowner pinch and scroll to find your number. Check every page on a phone before it goes live, and again after any change.

Test it now

Paste your homepage into Google's free PageSpeed Insights and look at the mobile tab. Then open the site on your own phone, outside on mobile data, and time how long it takes to find and tap your phone number.

Does the website help Google understand your business?

It should. Behind the visible page, a well-built site tells search engines in a structured way who you are, where you work and what you do, using the local business markup Google documents. Homeowners never see it. Google reads it on every page.
